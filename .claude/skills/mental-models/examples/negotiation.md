# Example: Negotiation

## Situation

User: "I'm negotiating a vendor contract renewal. They're a critical supplier and they know it. They're proposing a 22% price increase citing 'market conditions'. We can't easily switch — migration would take ~6 months. I feel cornered."

## Problem shape

Adversarial, asymmetric (they're stronger), and the user feels stuck. That's **Tree 3 (conflict)** on the weaker-party branch, crossed with **Tree 2 (stuck)**.

## Models picked (3, cross-category)

1. **m72 Asymmetric Warfare** (Strategy) — because head-on price negotiation is the strong party's game
2. **m19 Incentives** (Human Nature) — because "market conditions" is a stated reason, not necessarily the real one
3. **m63 Framing** (Art) — because "22% increase vs. 0%" is their frame, and accepting the frame is already losing

## Reasoning walkthrough

**Incentives — "What does the vendor actually want?"**
Price is the surface. Underneath:
- Quarterly revenue target from their sales rep (maybe a smaller increase + multi-year commit is better for them)
- Retention metric from their account team (churn is expensive; they'd rather not lose you)
- A benchmark they're trying to set for other customers (the "market conditions" framing is often about precedent, not cost)
- Possibly an internal pricing policy their rep can't break but can work around via add-ons/credits

Once you know which of these is live, you know what to trade. The price number is rarely the only variable.

**Framing — "Whose frame are we negotiating in?"**
Current frame: "accept 22% or migrate." Both options are bad. Reframes to try:
- Time frame: "22% this year, or 8% with a 3-year commitment" (shifts to a variable you control)
- Scope frame: "22% on current scope, or 0% with reduced scope / removed SKUs"
- Value frame: "what would justify 22%? what new value comes with it?" (forces them to defend the number as value, not market)
- Relationship frame: "we're evaluating strategic vendors this year — which tier do you want to be in?" (reintroduces future business as leverage)

The user isn't cornered; they're trapped inside a binary someone else wrote.

**Asymmetric Warfare — "Where is my hidden leverage?"**
You can't win on price or switching speed (their strengths). Where are they weak?
- They care about logo/reference value — offer/revoke a case study
- They care about expansion — offer/revoke introductions to other teams
- They care about precedent with other customers — a public win with you is worth $ to them
- You have 6 months of runway before switching — that's time to *credibly* start a parallel POC with an alternative, which changes their risk calculus even if you don't finish the migration
- Their sales rep has a quarter-end — timing is leverage

The game isn't "avoid 22%." The game is "change what's being measured."

## Conclusion

The user isn't cornered; they're inside the vendor's frame. The three models converge on: **stop negotiating the price, start negotiating the frame and the trade variables.**

Recommendation:
1. Do NOT counter with a number yet — every counter-number ratifies their frame
2. Ask discovery questions to surface the real incentive ("help me understand what's driving the 22% specifically")
3. Introduce two new variables (term length, scope) so there are more knobs to trade
4. Start a lightweight, *visible* alternative evaluation — credibility of the threat matters more than intent to migrate
5. Use timing: tie any agreement to their quarter-end for max rep urgency
6. Offer non-monetary value (case study, reference) as trade currency

Residual uncertainty: if they truly have no flexibility (rare), the fallback is the alternative evaluation becoming real — but that's a decision for month 3, not month 0.
