Munger put incentive-caused bias first among the causes of human misjudgment and said he had
underestimated it his whole life [^misjudgment]. The key word is *bias*, not corruption. The
salesperson who believes the product is right for you, the consultant who genuinely
concludes you need a bigger engagement, the engineer certain the rewrite is necessary — these
people are not lying. Their incentives shaped what they noticed, which evidence felt
compelling, and where they stopped looking. Sinclair's line captures it exactly: it is hard
to get someone to understand something when their salary depends on not understanding it
[^upton].

This is why it is the first model to reach for when behaviour looks irrational. Behaviour
that makes no sense given the stated goals usually makes perfect sense given the actual
rewards. "Why does nobody write tests?" is answered by looking at what gets someone promoted.

The uncomfortable half is that it applies to you, and from the inside, incentive-caused bias
is indistinguishable from being right.

## When to Avoid

- **As an accusation.** Stating that someone's view is bought is both insulting and usually
  wrong about the mechanism — they are biased, not dishonest, and treating it as dishonesty
  guarantees they stop listening.
- **When it explains everything.** If every disagreement is reducible to incentives, you have
  a theory that cannot be wrong and therefore tells you nothing. Some people just disagree.
- **When the incentive is weak or ambiguous.** Not every situation has strong enough
  incentives to bend judgement. Look for real magnitude before invoking it.
- **When you have not applied it to yourself first.** Using it exclusively outward is the
  most common failure, and it makes you less accurate, not more.

## Thinking Steps

1. **List who is affected by the decision.** Everyone with a stake, including yourself.
2. **For each, ask what they gain or lose.** Money, status, workload, risk of blame, an
  interesting project. Non-financial incentives are frequently the stronger ones.
3. **Predict their position from incentives alone.** Before hearing their argument, work out
   what conclusion their situation points toward.
4. **Compare prediction to actual position.** Where they match, weight the argument less and
   look for independent evidence. Where they differ, weight it more — someone arguing against
   their own interest is worth listening to.
5. **Run it on yourself.** What conclusion does your position point toward? Notice whether
   that is the one you hold.
6. **Fix the system, not the person.** If the incentive reliably produces the bad outcome,
   the incentive is the thing to change.

## Coaching Questions

- "Who benefits if we decide this way?"
- "What would I conclude if I were paid the way they're paid?"
- "What conclusion do my own incentives point me toward here?"
- "Is anyone here arguing against their own interest? Why aren't we listening harder?"
- "What behaviour are we actually rewarding, as opposed to the one we ask for?"
