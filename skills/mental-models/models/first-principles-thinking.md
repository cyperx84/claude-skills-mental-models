A first principle is a proposition that cannot be deduced from anything more basic [^first-principle].
Reasoning from first principles means decomposing a problem until you hit those, then building
back up — rather than reasoning by analogy, which is what we do almost all of the time.
Descartes made the method explicit: doubt everything that can be doubted, keep only what
survives, rebuild from there [^descartes].

Most thinking is analogical, and for good reason — it's fast and usually right. "How do
competitors price this?" gets you to an answer in minutes. But analogy inherits every
assumption baked into the thing you're copying, including the ones that were true for
someone else's constraints and were never true for yours. First principles is what you reach
for when the inherited answer keeps failing and nobody can say why.

The cost is real: it is slow, it is effortful, and it frequently reproduces the conventional
answer. That is not a failure. Arriving at the same conclusion by a route you can defend is
worth something the borrowed version never was.

## When to Avoid

- **When speed matters more than novelty.** Most decisions are not worth rebuilding from
  atoms. Analogy is the correct default; first principles is the exception you reach for.
- **When you lack the domain knowledge to identify the actual primitives.** Decomposition by
  a novice produces confident nonsense — you strip to what *you* think is fundamental, which
  is usually just a less examined assumption.
- **When the conventional answer encodes hard-won experience.** Building codes, aviation
  checklists and medical protocols look arbitrary from first principles. They are compressed
  accident reports. "Nobody can explain why this rule exists" is a reason to investigate, not
  to override.
- **As a status move.** Announcing that you reason from first principles while actually
  reasoning by analogy to a different, more flattering reference class is common and hard to
  spot from the inside.

## Thinking Steps

1. **Write the current answer and its justification.** Whatever you or the team currently
   believe, and specifically *why*. You cannot examine an assumption you haven't stated.
2. **Ask of each element: how do I know this?** Trace each claim to its source. Most will
   trace to "someone told me" or "that's how it's done" — those are the ones to attack.
3. **Strip to what survives.** Keep only what is physically, mathematically, or
   contractually necessary. Everything else is a choice someone made.
4. **Name the constraints you actually have.** Not the ones you inherited. Budget, physics,
   law, and time are constraints; convention and precedent are not.
5. **Rebuild.** Construct a solution from the surviving pieces, ignoring how it is normally
   done.
6. **Compare against the conventional answer.** If they match, you have earned confidence.
   If they differ, you have found either an opportunity or a constraint you missed — and
   working out which is the real work.

## Coaching Questions

- "What do we know is true here, as opposed to what we've assumed?"
- "How do we know that? Who told us, and how did they know?"
- "If this industry didn't exist and we started today, what would we build?"
- "Which of our constraints are physics and which are habit?"
- "What would have to be true for the conventional answer to be wrong?"
