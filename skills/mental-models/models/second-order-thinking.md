First-order thinking stops at the immediate consequence. Second-order thinking asks what
happens *after* that, and after that. Howard Marks framed it as the difference between
first-level thinking ("it's a good company, buy the stock") and second-level thinking ("it's
a good company, but everyone knows that, so it's overpriced — sell") [^marks]. The gap
between those two is where most of the available advantage lives, because first-order
conclusions are the ones everybody reaches.

The classic demonstration is the cobra effect: a bounty on cobra skins produced cobra
farming, and cancelling the bounty released the now-worthless snakes, leaving more cobras
than before the policy [^cobra]. Every step was locally rational. The failure was in stopping
the analysis one step too early.

Second-order effects are usually delayed, usually diffuse, and almost always where the real
cost sits. They are also where compounding lives, which is why the model cuts both ways:
the same discipline that reveals a hidden cost reveals a hidden payoff nobody else priced.

## When to Avoid

- **On trivial or easily reversed decisions.** Applying it to lunch is a waste of a scarce
  resource. Reserve it for decisions that are expensive to undo.
- **When it becomes an infinite regress.** There is always another order. Past the second or
  third, you are usually generating fiction, not analysis — the error bars exceed the signal.
- **When it produces paralysis.** Every action has some bad downstream consequence. If the
  model consistently returns "therefore do nothing," it is being used to rationalise
  inaction, not to evaluate it.
- **In genuinely chaotic systems.** Where small changes produce wildly divergent outcomes,
  detailed second-order prediction is false precision. Build for resilience instead of
  forecasting the specific consequence.

## Thinking Steps

1. **State the action and its intended first-order effect.** "Cut prices 20% → we sell more
   units."
2. **Ask "and then what?"** What does the world do in response? Competitors, customers,
   your own team.
3. **Ask it again.** The second iteration is where most of the value is. Competitors match →
   the volume gain evaporates → you've reset the price expectation permanently.
4. **Look specifically for the actor whose incentives you just changed.** Perverse outcomes
   almost always arrive through someone rationally responding to a new incentive.
5. **Check the time axis.** Name *when* each effect lands. First-order effects are usually
   fast and second-order slow, which is exactly why the fast one dominates the decision.
6. **Ask what the effect is if it works too well.** Many second-order disasters are the
   first-order success arriving at ten times the expected scale.

## Coaching Questions

- "And then what happens?"
- "Who changes their behaviour because of this, and how?"
- "What does this look like in two years if it works exactly as intended?"
- "What are we teaching people to do by doing this?"
- "If this succeeds beyond our expectations, what breaks?"
