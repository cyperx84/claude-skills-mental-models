Tversky and Kahneman gave people a choice between programmes to combat a disease expected to
kill 600. Described in lives saved, most chose the certain option; described in lives lost,
most chose the gamble — despite the options being arithmetically identical [^framing-effect].
The frame, not the facts, drove the decision.

The mechanism is that we evaluate outcomes as changes from a reference point rather than as
final states, and losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good
[^prospect]. Move the reference point and the same outcome becomes a win or a loss.

The practical use is defensive and offensive at once. Defensively: any decision you're
presented with arrived inside a frame someone chose, and re-describing it in the opposite
direction is a cheap check on whether your preference is about the substance. Offensively:
if you are communicating, you are framing whether you think about it or not, and the default
frame is rarely the best one.

## When to Avoid

- **When reframing becomes manipulation.** The line is whether the alternative frame is
  equally true. Choosing the honest frame that helps is persuasion; choosing the misleading
  one is deception, and the technique is identical.
- **When the reference point is genuinely correct.** Not all frames are arbitrary. If money
  really was spent, "sunk cost" is not a framing trick, it is a fact about the world.
- **When it substitutes for changing the substance.** Reframing a bad deal does not improve
  the deal. Organisations reach for the frame precisely when the substance is unfixable.
- **On people who have seen the reframe.** Once someone notices you are working the frame,
  it reads as spin and costs you the credibility that made it work.

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the current frame.** What is being treated as the baseline, and what counts as
   a gain or a loss relative to it?
2. **Restate it in the opposite direction.** Gains as losses, losses as gains. Same numbers.
3. **Check whether your preference survived.** If it flipped, your preference is about the
   frame and you have not actually decided anything yet.
4. **Ask who chose this frame and what it does for them.** Frames are rarely accidental.
5. **Find the reference point.** Change what counts as "normal" and the whole evaluation
   moves.
6. **If communicating, choose deliberately.** Pick the frame that is both true and clearest —
   and be able to state the one you rejected.

## Coaching Questions

- "How would this sound described as a loss instead of a gain?"
- "What are we treating as the baseline, and who picked it?"
- "If we'd never made the earlier investment, what would we choose now?"
- "Does our preference survive restating the same numbers the other way?"
- "Whose frame are we currently inside?"
