A lever multiplies force by trading distance for it — the further from the fulcrum, the more
the same push accomplishes [^lever]. Archimedes' claim about moving the world with a long
enough lever is the point: the input did not change, the placement did.

Meadows generalised this to systems and ranked where interventions actually work [^leverage-points].
Her finding was that people intuitively push on the lowest-leverage points — parameters,
numbers, budgets — because those are visible and adjustable, while the high-leverage points
are rules, information flows, goals and the paradigm underneath. Changing a number is easy
and rarely matters. Changing who gets to see what information is hard and frequently changes
everything.

The practical discipline is not working harder. It is refusing to apply effort until you have
located the place where effort converts into outcome at a better rate. Most of the difference
between people's output is not intensity, it is placement.

## When to Avoid

- **When leverage amplifies mistakes.** Leverage is directionally neutral. A high-leverage
  intervention in the wrong direction is worse than low-leverage flailing, and financial
  leverage is the standard demonstration.
- **When the fulcrum is not where you think.** Acting on an incorrect model of the system
  concentrates all your effort in one wrong place.
- **When the search costs more than the work.** For small tasks, finding the leverage point
  takes longer than just doing it.
- **When high-leverage points are politically unavailable.** Meadows noted the highest ones
  are the most defended. Identifying one you cannot touch can be worse than useless if it
  becomes a reason to do nothing.

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define the outcome you want to move.** Specific and measurable, or you cannot compare
   intervention points.
2. **List candidate intervention points.** Everything you could push on, including ones
   outside your authority.
3. **Estimate the ratio for each.** Effort in versus expected change out. Ratios, not
   absolute impact.
4. **Climb Meadows' ladder.** If your candidates are all parameters and budgets, look higher
   — at rules, at who has what information, at what the system is actually optimising for.
5. **Check the direction before applying force.** Ask what happens if you are wrong about the
   mechanism. High leverage means the error is amplified too.
6. **Apply and measure.** If the output does not move disproportionately, the fulcrum was
   wrong. Re-locate rather than pushing harder.

## Coaching Questions

- "Where's the smallest change that moves this the most?"
- "Are we pushing on a number when we could change a rule?"
- "Who has information they can't act on, and who acts without information?"
- "If we're wrong about the mechanism, how bad is applying force here?"
- "What is this system actually optimising for, as opposed to what we say?"
