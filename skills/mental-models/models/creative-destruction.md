Schumpeter described capitalism as a process of industrial mutation that "incessantly
revolutionises the economic structure from within, incessantly destroying the old one,
incessantly creating a new one" [^schumpeter]. Progress is not additive. The new thing takes
the old thing's place, and someone was employed by the old thing.

Christensen supplied the mechanism for why the incumbent, who can see it coming, loses
anyway [^christensen]. The new entrant starts worse on every axis the incumbent's best
customers care about. Listening to those customers — the thing good management does —
correctly says ignore it. By the time the entrant is good enough, the incumbent's cost
structure and organisation are built around the model being displaced.

Applied internally, this is why rewrites fail politically rather than technically. The
existing system has staff, budget and a track record; the replacement has a prototype. Every
local incentive protects what exists.

## When to Avoid

- **As a euphemism.** "Creative destruction" is often used to describe harm to other people
  as though it were weather. The destruction is real and lands on someone.
- **When the incumbent is simply better.** Not every new entrant is disruptive. Most are just
  worse, and the model gives a flattering story to a failing product.
- **When novelty becomes the goal.** Destroying a working system because destruction feels
  like progress is how organisations lose capability they cannot rebuild.
- **On short timescales.** These processes run over years. Using the model to justify a
  decision this quarter is usually motivated reasoning.

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify what currently works and who depends on it.** Revenue, staff, customers,
   internal processes. This is what the change would destroy.
2. **Find the thing that is worse but improving.** Ask what is currently dismissed as a toy,
   and at what rate it is getting better.
3. **Compare rates, not levels.** The question is never whether it is good enough today. It
   is whether its trajectory crosses your requirements before you can respond.
4. **Ask who inside would lose.** Name the budget, team or metric that shrinks if the new
   thing wins. That is where the resistance will come from, and it will be framed as risk.
5. **Check what your best customers are telling you.** If they are unanimous that the new
   thing is irrelevant, that is the exact signal Christensen described, not reassurance.
6. **Decide whether to cannibalise yourself.** Doing it deliberately is expensive. Having it
   done to you is worse.

## Coaching Questions

- "What are we currently protecting, and what does protecting it cost?"
- "What's the toy version of our product, and how fast is it improving?"
- "Who inside this company loses if the new approach wins?"
- "Are our best customers telling us to ignore this?"
- "If a competitor did this to us, how long would we have to respond?"
