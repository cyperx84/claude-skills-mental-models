Asymmetric conflict is between opponents whose resources and strategies differ so much that
conventional comparison stops predicting outcomes [^mack]. Mack's insight was that the
stronger side often loses not militarily but politically: for the weaker party the conflict
is existential, for the stronger it is optional, so the weaker can absorb far more pain
relative to its will to continue [^arreguin]. Victory is not defeating the opponent; it is
outlasting their interest.

Translated to business, the small competitor does not win by being a better version of the
incumbent — that is fighting on the incumbent's terms, where resources decide. It wins by
picking a dimension the incumbent cannot follow without damaging itself: a segment too small
to matter, a price that would cannibalise the main line, a support model that does not scale.

The defining constraint is the one people skip: the asymmetry has to be something the
opponent *cannot* copy, not merely something they have not copied. If they can match it next
quarter, it is a feature, not a strategy.

## When to Avoid

- **When you are the incumbent.** The model reads very differently from the strong side, and
  adopting insurgent tactics as the market leader usually reads as flailing.
- **When the asymmetry is copyable.** A cheaper price is not an asymmetry if they can cut
  prices. The advantage must be structurally unavailable to them.
- **When it invites disproportionate response.** Provoking a much stronger opponent who has
  not yet bothered to respond can end the game early.
- **When ethics are treated as another constraint to route around.** Deception and
  norm-breaking are available and cost credibility that is far harder to rebuild than
  revenue.

## Thinking Steps

1. **State the asymmetry precisely.** What do they have that you don't, and what do you have
   that they can't get — speed, focus, willingness to do unscalable things, nothing to lose.
2. **Identify what winning means for each side.** Different objectives is the core asymmetry.
   You may be able to win without them losing.
3. **Find what they cannot do without hurting themselves.** Their commitments are your
   opening: existing customers, margin structure, brand promises, public commitments.
4. **Refuse their metrics.** Competing on feature count against a company with fifty
   engineers is choosing the terrain where you lose.
5. **Check they cannot copy it.** If they can, it is a temporary feature. Keep looking.
6. **Work out how long you can sustain it.** Asymmetric strategies are endurance strategies.
   Know your runway before starting.

## Coaching Questions

- "What would it look like to refuse to compete on their terms?"
- "What can they not do without damaging their existing business?"
- "What can we do that doesn't scale — and would they even want to?"
- "If they wanted to copy this next quarter, could they?"
- "How long can we sustain this, and how long will they bother?"
