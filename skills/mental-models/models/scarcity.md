Economics treats scarcity as the basic condition: finite resources, competing uses, so
everything has a price and a trade-off [^scarcity-econ]. Mullainathan and Shafir found the
more useful version for decision-making — scarcity is a psychological state that changes how
well you think [^scarcity-book].

Being short of something captures attention. That produces a real benefit, the focus
dividend: people short on time hit deadlines, people short on money track prices with
unusual accuracy. It also produces tunnelling. Bandwidth goes to the scarce thing and comes
out of everything else, so the person under deadline neglects the relationship, and the
company short on cash stops investing in the thing that would end the shortage.

The compounding version is the scarcity trap: borrowing against the future to solve today's
shortage guarantees a worse shortage next period. A team that ships by taking on technical
debt has less capacity next quarter, which forces more debt. The trap is not stupidity, it is
the rational local response to a binding constraint.

## When to Avoid

- **When the resource is not actually scarce.** Manufactured urgency produces the tunnelling
  costs with none of the benefit. Check whether the deadline is real before reorganising
  around it.
- **As an explanation for others' decisions you dislike.** "They're tunnelling" is a
  convenient way to dismiss a choice made under constraints you are not carrying.
- **When abundance is the actual problem.** Too many options, too much budget and too much
  time produce their own failures, and the scarcity lens will not see them.
- **When it licenses a permanent emergency.** Organisations that explain every shortcut by
  scarcity have made scarcity a culture rather than a condition.

## Thinking Steps

1. **Name what is actually scarce.** Time, money, attention, headcount, political capital.
   Be specific; the stated scarcity is often not the binding one.
2. **Check whether it is real.** Distinguish a genuine constraint from an urgency someone
   manufactured.
3. **Ask what is being neglected.** Whatever is outside the tunnel — maintenance,
   relationships, the strategic work — is where the cost is landing.
4. **Look for the trap.** Is today's fix borrowing from next period? Name the repayment.
5. **Build slack deliberately.** Slack looks like waste under scarcity and it is what stops
   the trap compounding. It has to be designed in, because it will never survive triage.
6. **Decide the important things outside the tunnel.** Make consequential calls when you are
   not in the crunch, because judgement inside it is measurably worse.

## Coaching Questions

- "What's genuinely scarce here, and is the constraint real?"
- "What are we not thinking about because we're focused on this?"
- "Does today's fix make next quarter harder?"
- "Where's our slack, and what happened to it?"
- "Would we make this same call if we weren't under the gun?"
