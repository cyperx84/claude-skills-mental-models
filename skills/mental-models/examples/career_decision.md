# Example: Career Decision

## Situation

User: "I have an offer from a well-known startup at 30% more comp, but I'd be leaving a senior role at a stable company where I'm respected and shipping. I have 3 weeks to decide. I keep flip-flopping."

## Problem shape

High-stakes, low-reversibility, personal decision under uncertainty — and the user is stuck. That matches **Tree 1 (risk/reversibility)** and **Tree 2 (stuck)**. Reach across both.

## Models picked (3, cross-category)

1. **m07 Inversion** (General) — because the failure modes of a job move are more knowable than the upside
2. **m05 Second-Order Thinking** (General) — because "30% more comp" is a first-order framing
3. **m51 Trade-offs** (Economics) — because the user is flip-flopping, which usually means an unnamed cost

Note: We skip m02 Circle of Competence — the user isn't questioning their ability, they're questioning fit.

## Reasoning walkthrough

**Inversion — "How do I guarantee this move is a disaster?"**
- Accept purely for comp without validating the team, manager, or product-market fit
- Leave behind relationships that took years to build, right before they'd pay off
- Arrive as an unknown senior at a place that already has a pecking order
- Join at a stage where the 30% premium is priced for the risk, not a gift

Already one insight: the 30% is *risk-adjusted comp*, not free money. That reframes the whole offer.

**Second-Order — "Comp is up 30%. And then what?"**
- First-order: more money, new logo
- Second-order: in 18 months, either the startup works (illiquid equity, burnout, strong brand) or it doesn't (job search from a weaker position, one-year stint on resume)
- Third-order: the user's *network* at the current company compounds; starting over resets that compounding

**Trade-offs — "What does each option actually cost?"**
- Staying costs: ceiling on learning rate, possible regret, forgone 30%
- Leaving costs: reputation capital at current co, shipping momentum, the *respected* part — which the user mentioned unprompted
- Flip-flopping usually means one cost is felt but unnamed. Here it's "being respected" — that's the hidden variable.

## Conclusion

The three models converge on the same diagnosis: the user isn't weighing comp vs. comp, they're weighing *compounded reputation capital* vs. *optionality on a risky bet*. The 30% is not the real number; the real question is "do I believe this startup enough that I'd take the offer at +0%?"

Actionable next steps to give the user:
1. Run the inversion list as a diligence checklist with the startup (talk to 3 engineers who left, ask about the manager, see the actual product metrics)
2. Reframe the comp as risk-adjusted — mentally subtract the premium and ask if it's still exciting
3. Name the hidden cost ("being respected and shipping") and check whether it's portable or bound to the current context

Residual uncertainty: only the user knows their risk tolerance and life stage. The models clarify the question; they don't answer it.
